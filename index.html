The problem

One officer, ten cameras, no one at the weekend.

Council's end-of-pipe litter nets catch bottles, bags and leaves before they reach the creeks and the river. When a full net meets a storm, it is built to tear off, and everything it caught goes downstream.

Today one stormwater officer checks about ten working cameras by eye, one photo a day, on weekdays only. Of the 25 sites on Council's camera wall, 11 show no image at all. Nets are cleaned every quarter and after big rain, so half-empty nets get emptied and full ones get missed.

How it works

From camera to clean, with a person at every gate.

  1. 01

    Read the camera

    Each daily frame is checked for full, torn or blocked nets, with a confidence. Unsure frames go to a person. Our first model is tested in the app.

  2. 02

    Add the rain

    Live FloodSmart gauges and a 72-hour forecast estimate which nets fill, and which creeks rise, before the storm.

  3. 03

    Rank every net

    One priority score: condition, rain, criticality and build-up.

  4. 04

    Officer approves

    A morning email, and an urgent email when a net tears. One click raises the job; the contractor's photo and the officer's sign-off close it.

Built responsibly

The AI ranks. People decide.

We tested a first image model on eight Council frames it had never seen. It got one right. That is why Net Watch only ranks nets, sends unsure frames to a person, and never raises a job on its own.

See the model checkSee the data register
  • Human at every gateNo work order without an officer. Officers can correct a reading, remove a frame or pause auto-flagging. Every step is logged.
  • Demo data is labelledFill levels and job history are dummy values and marked as such wherever they appear.
  • No personal dataBushland frames only, road blurred, resident reports carry no names, officers appear by role.
  • Built on what Council hasSCADA camera frames, FloodSmart gauges, the asset register and the cleaning contract. No new sensors for the pilot.
